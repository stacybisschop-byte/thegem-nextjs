# The Secret Language of Gems: Inside the World of Acrostic Rings

> **In Brief:** Acrostic rings spell out words using the first letter of each gemstone's name — DEAREST becomes Diamond, Emerald, Amethyst, Ruby, Emerald, Sapphire, Turquoise. Invented in Georgian Paris and adored by the Victorians, they turned a ring into a cipher only the wearer could decode.

I own a ring that says nothing. That's the point of most rings, admittedly, but this one is a plain gold band set with a single sapphire, and it has never once tried to tell me it loves me. Acrostic rings do the opposite. They gossip. They confess. They spell things out in stone the way a note passed under a desk spells things out in ink, and I find that a far more interesting proposition than a solitaire ever managed.

An acrostic ring is a piece of jewellery in which the first letter of each gemstone, read in order around the band, spells a word. The mechanism is disarmingly simple and the effect is not: a row of stones that looks, to an untrained eye, like ordinary decorative variety is in fact a sentence. Diamond, Emerald, Amethyst, Ruby, Emerald, Sapphire, Turquoise reads DEAREST. Lapis, Opal, Vermeil garnet, Emerald reads LOVE, more or less, depending on which jeweller's cipher you consult, since the alphabet of stones was never perfectly standardised.

## History

The convention is generally traced to Jean-Baptiste Mellerio, jeweller to Marie Antoinette's circle, who is said to have set a ring with stones spelling J'ADORE. The French court, characteristically, went wild for it. Napoleon apparently commissioned pieces of this kind for Joséphine and other family members, which tells you something about the Napoleonic taste for symbolism dressed as sentiment.

By the Victorian era the fashion had crossed the Channel and settled into English jewellery boxes with real staying power. REGARD and DEAREST were the two workhorse words, common enough that a jeweller could keep the requisite stones in stock. REGARD used ruby, emerald, garnet, amethyst, ruby, diamond — a neat six-letter word built almost entirely from stones any workshop already had to hand, which probably explains why it survives in such numbers today. The Victorians liked their sentiment legible only to the initiated, and an acrostic ring let a woman wear a declaration in full view of a drawing room while nobody in that drawing room could read it. It's the same instinct, dressed in a different mood, that runs through [Victorian mourning jewellery](/stories/victorian-mourning-jewellery): a private message coded into something you could wear at dinner without a word of explanation.

Rarer and more interesting are the political variants. Suffrage-era jewellers produced pieces spelling SUFFRAGE and REPEAL, using the same gem-cipher logic to smuggle a political position into a piece of jewellery a woman could wear without a word being spoken. These are scarce now, and desirable for exactly the reason they were made: a ring that says something you were not, at the time, meant to say aloud.

## How the Cipher Works

The system was never governed by a single official dictionary, which is why you'll find slight variations between pieces, but the core alphabet held reasonably steady across the major jewellery houses:

- **A:** Amethyst
- **B:** Beryl
- **D:** Diamond
- **E:** Emerald
- **G:** Garnet
- **J:** Jasper
- **L:** Lapis lazuli
- **O:** Opal
- **R:** Ruby
- **S:** Sapphire
- **T:** Topaz or Turquoise
- **U:** Uranite or Ural emerald
- **V:** Verde antique or Vermeil garnet

Trickier letters (C, F, H, K) forced jewellers into inventive substitutions or simply steered the whole enterprise toward words that avoided them, which is one reason the surviving vocabulary of acrostic jewellery is so narrow. Nobody was spelling out anything with a K in it. The same coded impulse shows up a few decades earlier in [floral jewellery](/stories/floral-jewellery), where flowers rather than gemstone initials carried the hidden meaning — a related Georgian habit of making sentiment illegible to anyone who hadn't been let in on it.

If you're curious about the older folklore behind any of these individual stones, beyond their role as letters, [Grimoire](https://getgrimoire.app) covers the meaning and history of each one in more depth.

## Buying One Today

Prices vary considerably, and the variables are the ones you'd expect: age, condition, rarity of the word, and whether the piece carries documented provenance. A plain Georgian or Victorian REGARD ring in decent condition tends to sit around the £700–£900 mark. Rarer sentiments, unusual stone arrangements (a flower-shaped setting instead of a plain band, say), or a documented history can push a piece to £2,500–£4,000. The political rarities command a premium disproportionate to their size, on account of how few survive.

If you're buying, ask the dealer to confirm the reading before you commit. Stones get reset, rings get repaired by people with no idea what they're looking at, and a ring that once spelled DEAREST can quietly lose an emerald and gain a peridot in its place, which rather ruins the joke. Treat the reading with the same scrutiny you'd give [a hallmark](/guides/jewellery-hallmarks-uk) on any other antique gold purchase: a dealer who specialises in antique and Georgian jewellery is more likely to have checked than a general antiques shop.

Contemporary jewellers have picked the format back up too, offering bespoke acrostic pieces built to spell a name or a private word of the buyer's choosing. It's a legitimate way to get a personalised piece with a genuine historical pedigree behind the idea, not a novelty invented last Tuesday for the personalisation trend.

## Modern Acrostic Jewellery: Where to Buy

The format never really died, though for a long stretch it went quiet. It's back now, largely as a bespoke commission instead of an off-the-shelf line, which suits a piece of jewellery whose entire point is that nobody else could possibly own the same message.

**[Sophie Breitmeyer](https://sophiebreitmeyer.com/products/acrostic-pendant)** has relaunched an Acrostic Collection out of London, working in 9k yellow gold with up to seven coloured stones per piece, engraved lines standing in for the coded letters. It's the closest thing on the current market to a proper English continuation of the Georgian original, and it's the one I'd point a Gem reader toward first.

**[Erica Weiner](https://www.ericaweiner.com/products/secret-message-acrostic-ring-2021)**, the American jeweller known for quietly clever heirloom-adjacent pieces, offers a made-to-order acrostic ring with an interactive stone-picker, available in white, rose or yellow gold. It's a slower process (expect six to eight weeks) and the piece is final sale once commissioned, but the result is a proper gold ring, not a novelty item.

**[Lulu Frost](https://www.lulufrost.com/pages/our-collections-1)** builds the same idea under the name "Code" instead of acrostic, which is either a rebrand or a dodge depending on how charitable you're feeling, but the mechanism is identical: gemstones chosen to spell something private.

**[Violet Gray Design](https://violetgraydesign.com/products/acrostic-ring)**, a small Australian studio run entirely by its founder, makes acrostic rings to order with the kind of one-to-one attention you'd expect from a maker who isn't running a factory.

For something less considered and more immediate, [Etsy](https://www.etsy.com/market/custom_acrostic_jewelry) has a very large number of independent jewellers offering custom acrostic and birthstone pieces. Quality varies wildly stand to stand, so check reviews and ask for photographs of previous acrostic commissions specifically, since a shop that's good at birthstone rings isn't automatically good at building a coded sentence out of stones.

## Antique Dealers Worth Visiting

If you want the real thing instead of a modern commission, London remains one of the best places in the world to go looking, and you don't need an appointment to start.

**[Grays Antique Market](https://www.graysantiques.com/)**, on Davies Street in Mayfair, just around the corner from Bond Street station, is the obvious starting point. Close to 100 specialist dealers trade across two floors of a converted Edwardian building, and jewellery is very much the house speciality, with strong holdings in Georgian, Victorian, Edwardian and Art Deco pieces. [DB Gems](https://www.dbgems.com/), based within Grays, has a particular reputation for fine antique and vintage jewellery and is worth a stop. Go on a weekday morning if you want the dealers' full attention instead of a Saturday crowd.

**[Alfies Antique Market](https://alfiesantiques.com/)** in Marylebone is Grays' sister centre and absorbed a number of dealers when part of the original Davies Mews site closed, so it's worth the short trip north if Grays doesn't turn anything up.

**Hatton Garden** remains London's jewellery quarter proper — [our guide to shopping it](/guides/hatton-garden-guide) covers how to navigate the district — and specialist antique dealers there, **[Berganza](https://www.berganza.com/)** among them, deal specifically in Georgian and Victorian pieces including acrostic rings when they surface. It's a more formal shopping experience than Grays, better suited to a considered purchase than a browse.

**[Wartski](https://wartski.com/)**, though best known for Fabergé, has handled acrostic pieces in the past and is worth a call if you're after something with real provenance and don't mind paying for it.

Wherever you go, the advice from earlier stands: ask the dealer to read the ring's message back to you before you buy, and get it in writing if the price reflects rarity or provenance.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does an acrostic ring spell?

Whatever word its stones were chosen to spell — the first letter of each gemstone's name is read in sequence around the ring. REGARD and DEAREST are the two most commonly found on the antique market.

### How can I tell if my ring is a genuine acrostic piece?

Check whether the first letters of the stone names, read in order, form a real word. Be cautious of rings that have been reset or repaired, since a missing or swapped stone can break the message entirely.

### Are acrostic rings only Victorian?

No. The tradition began earlier, in Georgian-era Paris, and continued through the Victorian period. Contemporary jewellers now also make bespoke versions to order.

### What's the difference between an acrostic ring and a birthstone ring?

A birthstone ring uses a single stone tied to a birth month. An acrostic ring uses a sequence of different stones chosen specifically for the letters they spell, which is a different exercise altogether.
