# Signet Ring Shapes: Oval, Cushion, Oxford, and Which Suits Which Hand

Most people choose a signet shape the way they'd choose a photograph: they look at a picture of the ring, decide they like it, and buy it. Then it arrives, and on their own hand it looks nothing like it did in the product shot. This isn't bad luck or a flaw in the ring. A signet ring sits directly against the widest, most visible part of the finger, closer to the eye than almost any other jewellery you'll wear, which means its shape reads against your hand in a way a necklace or a bracelet never has to. Two people can buy the identical ring in the identical size and end up with two different objects, because the hands underneath them are different.

Rebus and Hancocks, the two Hatton Garden and Bond Street houses that between them have made more signets than almost anyone in Britain, both offer a way to check the fit before committing: printable sizing guides, resin replicas, a chance to see the actual proportions against your own knuckle before a maker starts cutting gold. That's worth paying attention to, because it tells you shape-to-hand fit is a real and recurring problem, not a nicety.

> *In Brief: The two classic signet shapes are the Oxford oval (a vertical oval, the most popular shape in the category) and the cushion (a rounded rectangle with a squarer, bolder face). Landscape oval, round, marquise, and shield are the other names you'll encounter. Longer, narrower fingers generally suit the more substantial cushion or shield without looking overwhelmed; shorter or wider fingers usually read better in an oval, which elongates instead of adding width. Face size should scale with your hand, not with how the ring looked on someone else's.*

## The shape families, properly named

The signet market uses a handful of names consistently, and it's worth knowing them before you start looking, because "oval" alone doesn't tell a maker much.

**Oxford oval.** The classic, and the bestselling shape at most serious makers. A vertical oval, narrower at the sides than it is tall, oriented to sit along the length of the finger. The name comes from its long use at Oxford and Cambridge for college and family crests, and it remains the shape most people picture when they picture a signet ring at all.

**Landscape oval.** The same oval, turned ninety degrees, wider than it is tall. Better suited to crests or monograms that are naturally horizontal in composition, and a slightly less traditional choice.

**Cushion.** A rounded rectangle: squarer corners, bowed sides, more surface area than an oval of the same width. It reads as a heavier, more deliberate shape, and gives an engraver more room to work with, which is why cushion faces are often preferred for detailed heraldry.

**Round.** Exactly what it sounds like, and historically less common than the oval or cushion, though it has become more popular as a softer, more contemporary alternative to both.

**Shield.** Tapers toward the base, widening at the top, in the manner of an actual heraldic shield. The obvious choice for a crest and an odd one for anything else, since the shape itself signals coat of arms before the engraving does.

**Marquise.** A pointed oval, sharper at both ends. The most contemporary of the group, and the one furthest from the ring's ceremonial origins.

## Reading your own hand

This is the part most buying guides skip, and it's the actual decision.

**Finger length relative to width.** A short, wide finger carries a substantial cushion or shield without difficulty, because there's enough length to support a face that reads as bold, not crowded. The same cushion on a long, narrow finger can look like it's sitting on a stick: technically correctly sized, visually wrong. On a narrower finger, the oval is doing real work, not just following tradition. Its elongated shape draws the eye along the finger instead of across it, which makes the finger read as proportional instead of dominated by the ring.

**Knuckle width versus finger base.** Signet rings sit between the base knuckle and the first knuckle, and the gap between those two widths determines how a ring behaves in practice, not just how it looks. If your knuckle is noticeably wider than the base of the finger, a heavier or more rectangular face like the cushion or shield can look proportionate at rest but be awkward to get over the knuckle when putting the ring on or taking it off. A more tapered shape, or a slightly smaller face, avoids that friction.

**Hand size overall.** A large hand can carry a substantial cushion or shield at the top of a maker's size range without it reading as costume jewellery. The identical ring on a smaller hand usually needs to come down a size or two, or move to an oval, so it doesn't become the only thing anyone notices. This isn't about gender — women's hands and men's hands both range widely in size, and the calculation is the same either way: proportion to the hand you actually have, not to a size that felt satisfying to ask for.

**What the shape does optically.** As a rule that holds up across most hands: ovals elongate, cushions and shields add visual weight, round softens. If your instinct is uncertain, start from the effect you want, not which shape you've seen most often, since the second is mostly a function of the Oxford oval's popularity, not proof it suits every hand equally.

## Face size, in real numbers

Hancocks offers its Oxford oval across four standard face sizes: 10mm × 8mm, 12mm × 10mm, 14mm × 12mm, and 16mm × 13mm, measured edge to edge. That range is worth having in mind even if you're buying elsewhere, because it gives you a concrete sense of scale instead of a vague "small, medium, large."

As a starting point: the two smaller sizes suit narrower fingers or a first signet bought for restraint over statement. The two larger sizes need a hand with the base width to carry them, and are where a shield or cushion earns its heavier reputation. If you're between sizes and unsure, sizing down is the safer error. An oversized signet reads as a costume piece far more readily than an undersized one reads as timid.

## Shape and what you're putting on it

Shape and engraving aren't independent decisions, and it's worth choosing them together instead of in sequence.

A detailed crest, with a helm, mantling, and supporters, needs the extra surface area a cushion or shield provides; the same crest crammed onto a narrow Oxford oval will lose detail an engraver can't recover. A simple monogram or a single initial does the opposite: it can look lost on a large cushion face and settled on a smaller oval or round, where the letterform has less empty space to compete with. If you already know what you're engraving before you've chosen a shape, work backwards from the engraving's own proportions instead of picking a shape you like and hoping the design fits it.

## Quick reference

| Shape | Best suited to | Engraving it favours |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Oxford oval | Narrower or shorter fingers; the most versatile default | Monograms, single initials, simple crests |
| Landscape oval | Horizontally composed monograms or crests | Cyphers, wide motifs |
| Cushion | Wider fingers, larger hands, a bolder look | Detailed crests, coats of arms |
| Round | Any hand; the softest, most contemporary option | Single initials, small motifs |
| Shield | Larger hands; explicitly heraldic intent | Family crests, coats of arms |
| Marquise | Narrower fingers; a contemporary alternative to the oval | Simple monograms, motifs |

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the most popular signet ring shape?

The Oxford oval, by a clear margin at most established makers. It's the shape most people picture by default when they think of a signet ring, and both Rebus and Hancocks cite it as their bestselling design. Its popularity is partly tradition and partly practicality: the elongated oval suits a wider range of hands than a cushion or shield does.

### What signet ring shape suits a smaller hand?

The Oxford oval or the round, both at the smaller end of a maker's face-size range, generally suit smaller hands best. Their proportions elongate instead of adding width, which keeps the ring from dominating a narrower finger. A cushion or shield can still work on a smaller hand at a correspondingly smaller face size, but the visual weight of those shapes makes them harder to get right at scale.

### What is the difference between an Oxford oval and a landscape oval?

Both are the same oval shape, oriented differently. The Oxford oval runs vertically, taller than it is wide, along the length of the finger, and is the more traditional and more common orientation. The landscape oval is the same shape turned ninety degrees, wider than it is tall, and suits engravings that are naturally horizontal in composition instead of vertical.

### Why do signet rings come in a shield shape?

The shield shape directly references heraldic shields and is used almost exclusively for family crests and coats of arms, since the shape itself signals heraldic intent before the engraving is even visible. It tapers toward the base and widens toward the top, following the traditional proportions of an actual coat of arms, which gives a detailed crest more room than an oval face of the same width would.

### How do I know what signet ring face size to order?

Reputable makers offer either a printable sizing guide or a physical replica service, both worth using before committing to a bespoke piece in precious metal. As a general reference, standard Oxford oval faces run from around 10mm × 8mm at the smallest to 16mm × 13mm at the largest. The right size depends on the width of your finger at the base, not just your ring size, since two people with the same ring size can have noticeably different finger widths.
