# Garnet Buying Guide: The Birthstone That Comes in Six Colours

Until the late 1990s, gemmologists said garnet came in every colour except blue. It was one of those tidy, textbook facts, repeated in gem guides for a century, right up until a handful of small, strange pebbles turned up in Bekily, in southern Madagascar, and did the one thing garnet supposedly couldn't. They were blue in daylight, shifting to purple under a lamp, and nobody had catalogued anything quite like them before. Garnet, it turned out, had simply been saving its last colour.

That story is the whole argument for this guide. Garnet isn't a single gemstone with one quality ladder, the way a sapphire or a ruby is. It's a family of related minerals that happen to share a crystal structure and a name, and which between them cover more of the colour wheel than almost any other gem group on earth. Buying "a garnet" without knowing which one is a bit like buying "a cheese."

> *In Brief: garnet, January's birthstone, is a mineral group, not a single species, and different garnets (pyrope, almandine, spessartine, grossular, andradite, uvarovite) produce red, orange, green, pink-purple, colour-changing and, since a late-1990s Madagascar discovery, blue stones. Unlike sapphire and ruby, most garnet reaches the market untreated, which is one of its genuine advantages as a buy. Hardness runs roughly 6.5 to 7.5 on the Mohs scale, durable enough for daily wear in most varieties. Expect roughly £10–£40 per carat for commercial red garnet, into the low hundreds for fine rhodolite and spessartine, and £1,000 to well over £5,000 per carat for the finest tsavorite, demantoid and colour-change stones.*

## Garnet is a family, and the family decides everything

Six garnet species dominate the gem trade, and the differences between them aren't subtle. Pyrope and almandine produce the classic reds, with pyrope running toward a pure, fiery red and almandine trending darker and more brownish or violet-toned; a blend of the two, rhodolite, gives a lighter raspberry or purplish-pink that's become one of the most popular garnets on its own merits. Spessartine produces vivid orange stones, sometimes called mandarin garnet at their brightest. Grossular is the most colour-diverse species of all, running from colourless through yellow to the vivid green of tsavorite and the warm orange-brown of hessonite. Andradite gives demantoid, a brilliant green variety prized by collectors above almost any other garnet. Uvarovite is a bright emerald green, rarely cut because it typically forms in crystals too small for faceting.

And then there's colour-change garnet, usually a pyrope-spessartine blend, which shifts from green or blue-green in daylight to red or purple under incandescent light, the same optical trick that makes alexandrite famous, at a fraction of alexandrite's price. The rarest of these, the Bekily blue garnets, are coloured by vanadium and remain, more than two decades after their discovery, one of the hardest gemstones in the world to actually find for sale, especially above a carat.

## Clarity works differently depending which garnet you're holding

Red garnets, pyrope, almandine and rhodolite, are typically clean stones, and an eye-visible inclusion in one of these is a genuine mark against it. Orange garnets behave differently: spessartine and hessonite often carry visible inclusions as a simple fact of how they form, and buyers shouldn't read the same degree of concern into them.

Demantoid is the exception worth knowing by name. Some of its finest examples contain fine, curved golden fibres called horsetail inclusions, and GIA notes these can actually raise a demantoid's value rather than lower it. They're a classic signature of natural demantoid, associated above all with Russian stones, though they also turn up in material from Italy and Iran, and not every Russian demantoid has them.

## Cut matters more for some species than others

Demantoid in particular is often cut to exact proportions chosen to maximise its fire, the flashes of spectral colour the stone is capable of throwing, sometimes at some cost to carat weight retention; a well-cut smaller demantoid can be worth more than a larger, poorly proportioned one. Tsavorite and rhodolite reward the same general rule that applies across coloured stones: even colour across the face of the stone, with no dead windows or overly dark extinction, beats raw size every time.

## Origin, and why it changes the price more than the name does

**Red garnet** (pyrope, almandine and their rhodolite blend) comes from a wide spread of sources, including India, Sri Lanka, Brazil, and East Africa, and is correspondingly the most affordable and widely available corner of the family.

**Tsavorite**, the vivid green grossular variety, comes almost entirely from East Africa, principally Kenya and Tanzania, and carries real scarcity value as one of the few green gemstones that actually rivals emerald's colour without emerald's fragility.

**Demantoid** traces its reputation to Russia's Ural Mountains, where it was first found in 1851 and which are still considered the source of the finest examples, though a significant deposit discovered in Madagascar around 2009 has broadened supply. Namibia's Green Dragon mine, found in 1996, also produces demantoid.

**Spessartine**, or mandarin garnet at its most saturated, comes chiefly from Nigeria and Mozambique today, having originally been named for the Spessart region of Germany.

**Colour-change and blue garnet** are concentrated mainly in Madagascar and East Africa, with the Bekily deposit remaining the benchmark source for blue even as supply has grown increasingly sporadic.

## Treatment: the pleasant surprise in this cluster

Here's where garnet earns its place as the easiest buy of the stones covered in this autumn's cluster of guides. Sapphire and ruby are routinely heated; opal is sometimes smoke- or sugar-treated to darken its body tone, as our [opal buying guide](https://thegem.press/guides/opal-buying-guide) explains. Garnet, by contrast, reaches the market largely untreated. Its natural clarity and colour are typically strong enough that there's little commercial reason to enhance it.

The one documented exception is demantoid, where reports have circulated in the trade since around 2003 of low-temperature heat treatment used to improve colour in some Russian material; the treatment is thought to be difficult to detect and is not universal. For every other garnet species in ordinary commercial circulation, "untreated" is the reasonable default assumption, which is a rare thing to be able to say about a coloured stone.

## Certification: when it's worth asking for

For commercial garnet under a few hundred pounds, certification is rarely necessary; the stone is what it looks like, and the risk of a costly misrepresentation is low. For fine demantoid, tsavorite, or any colour-change or blue garnet, a report from GIA or another major lab is worth the cost, both to confirm species (since "rhodolite" in particular gets used loosely in the trade for various purplish-red garnets that aren't the true pyrope-almandine blend) and, for demantoid, to address treatment status directly.

## FAQ

**Is garnet one gemstone or several?** Several. Garnet is a mineral group covering at least six commercially important species, pyrope, almandine, spessartine, grossular, andradite and uvarovite, which together produce red, orange, green, pink-purple and colour-changing stones. "A garnet" could mean a £10-a-carat commercial red stone or a demantoid at several thousand pounds a carat; the species is what actually determines the answer.

**Is garnet treated like sapphire or ruby?** Generally no, and that's one of garnet's real advantages. Most garnet reaches the market untreated, unlike sapphire and ruby, which are routinely heated. The one documented exception is some Russian demantoid, which has reportedly been low-temperature heat treated since around 2003 to improve colour.

**What is rhodolite garnet?** A naturally occurring blend of pyrope and almandine, producing a lighter, raspberry or purplish-pink colour distinct from either parent species' pure red. It's one of the most popular garnets in its own right, prized for strong transparency and a colour few other affordable stones offer.

**Is blue garnet real?** Yes, though it's extremely rare. Natural blue garnet was discovered in the late 1990s at Bekily in southern Madagascar: a pyrope-spessartine colour-change variety, coloured by vanadium, that shifts from blue-green in daylight to purple under incandescent light. It remains one of the scarcest gemstones in the trade, particularly above a carat.

**What are horsetail inclusions, and are they a problem?** They're fine, curved golden fibre inclusions found in some demantoid garnet, and in this specific case they can raise the stone's value rather than lower it, since they're a classic signature of natural demantoid, especially Russian material.

**Is garnet durable enough for an everyday ring?** Most varieties are: garnet sits at roughly 6.5 to 7.5 on the Mohs hardness scale, comparable to tourmaline and harder than opal or tanzanite, which makes pyrope, almandine, rhodolite and tsavorite all reasonable choices for daily wear. Demantoid, at the softer end of that range, is better suited to earrings or an occasional-wear ring.
