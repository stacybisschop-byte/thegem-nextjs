# The Jewellery of Grief: What We Wear When Someone Dies

There's a particular kind of question that arrives in the first few days after someone dies, usually alongside far larger ones, and it can feel strange to be thinking about it at all: what happens to their jewellery, and what do you wear yourself. It isn't a frivolous question. Jewellery is one of the few things a person leaves behind that was actually touching them, and deciding what to do with it, whether that's wearing a piece as it is, having something made from it, or simply putting it away for now, is part of working out what you're carrying forward.

This isn't a history of mourning customs, though [that's an interesting subject in its own right](/stories/victorian-mourning-jewellery) if you want the Victorian conventions this piece deliberately leaves behind, and it isn't about symbolic mortality jewellery either, which [we've covered separately](/edit/how-to-wear-a-memento-mori). This is about the practical questions in front of you now: what's appropriate to wear, what your options actually are if you want something made, and what to watch for if you do.

> *In Brief: There's no fixed rule today about what to wear to a funeral or how soon you can wear a loved one's own jewellery; both are personal decisions, not etiquette to get wrong. If you're considering having something made from their ashes or hair, options range from simple engraved pieces from around £50 to cremation diamonds costing several thousand pounds, and it's worth knowing that not every provider in that second category is transparent about how much of the diamond actually comes from your loved one.*

## What to wear to the funeral

There's no longer a fixed dress code for mourning jewellery, the way there was in the Victorian period, and that's mostly a relief, not a loss. The general instinct that still holds is understatement: quiet pieces over statement ones, so the jewellery isn't the thing anyone notices. Pearls, small studs, a plain band, a watch you already wear daily; all of it is appropriate. There's no rule against colour or sentiment either. Plenty of people choose to wear something that belonged to the person they've lost, or something that reminds them specifically of them, and there's nothing wrong with that reading as personal instead of sombre.

If you're not sure what's expected at a specific service, the safest approach is simply to keep things quiet and let anything with meaning to you be the exception, not the rule for the day.

## Wearing their jewellery

There's no waiting period, whatever it might feel like there should be. Some people wear a piece the day after a death. Some people put it away for years before they're ready. Both are normal, and neither is more correct than the other.

A few practical things worth knowing if you do decide to wear an inherited piece. A ring sized for someone else's hand can be resized by most jewellers without losing anything of the original piece, provided the resizing is modest. A well-loved chain or clasp is worth having checked before you wear it daily, simply because it may not have been serviced in years. And if a piece needs cleaning, a reputable jeweller can do this without altering it in any way; it's a service, not an irreversible decision, whatever it might feel like in the moment.

## Having something made

This is the category most people haven't thought about until they need to, and it's worth understanding the real range of what's available before committing to any one option.

**Ashes or hair sealed into a piece.** The most established option: a small amount of ashes or hair sealed into a pendant, ring, or bracelet, either visibly (fused into coloured glass, for instance) or invisibly within the metal itself. UK workshops making these by hand, such as [Ashes With Art](https://www.asheswithart.co.uk) in West Sussex and [Chris Parry's](https://chris-parry-handmade.co.uk) Kent studio, typically start from around £100 for simpler silver pieces, moving up with gold, platinum, and more complex settings. Most ask for a small portion of ashes, often as little as a teaspoon, and return anything unused.

**Cremation diamonds.** A lab-grown diamond made from carbon extracted from ashes or hair, priced from roughly £800 to £10,000 or more depending on carat and cut. This is where it's worth pausing before you commit to anything. Independent reporting, including from jewellers working in the wider memorial trade, has raised genuine questions about how much of the finished diamond actually originates from the ashes submitted, since cremated remains alone don't always contain enough recoverable carbon to grow a diamond of any real size. Some providers are explicit that additional, non-ash carbon is used in the process; not all of them make this clear upfront. [Algordanza](https://www.algordanza.co.uk), for instance, states directly that it's the only provider guaranteeing 100% of the diamond's carbon comes from the remains submitted, which is itself worth noting: if that's a meaningful point of difference for one provider to make, it tells you the practice varies across the category. If you're considering this route, ask directly what proportion of the diamond's carbon comes from your loved one's remains specifically, and ask for that answer in writing before you pay a deposit. A provider unwilling to answer plainly is telling you something.

**Fingerprint or thumbprint pieces.** A cast of an actual fingerprint, taken either before a death where possible or from existing prints, pressed into a ring or pendant. Considerably simpler and cheaper than ashes or diamond work, typically starting well under £200, and for many people just as meaningful, since it's a physical trace of the person instead of a symbolic one.

**Birthstone and engraved pieces.** The simplest and most accessible option: a piece set with the person's birthstone, or engraved with a name, date, or short inscription. No ashes or hair required, widely available from almost any jeweller, and often the right choice if the idea of sending remains away for processing doesn't sit comfortably, which is a completely reasonable thing to feel.

## Questions worth asking before you commission anything

Whichever route you're considering, a few questions are worth asking any provider directly, in writing, before paying a deposit.

**What happens to the ashes or hair I send that isn't used?** A reputable provider returns anything unused instead of disposing of it.

**What's your turnaround time, honestly?** Cremation diamonds in particular can take six months to a year. Ask for a realistic timeline, not a best-case one.

**Is the metal hallmarked?** The same UK hallmarking standards apply to memorial jewellery as anything else. A piece over one gram should carry a proper hallmark, and a maker unwilling to confirm this is worth being cautious of.

**For a cremation diamond specifically: what proportion of the carbon comes from the remains I send?** This is the single most important question in this entire category, for the reasons above.

## Frequently asked questions

### How soon can you wear a deceased loved one's jewellery?

Whenever feels right to you. There's no waiting period and no rule you're at risk of breaking either way. Some people wear a piece within days; others wait years, or never do, and all of these are equally normal responses to grief.

### Is it disrespectful to wear coloured or statement jewellery to a funeral?

Not by any current convention. Understated pieces are the general instinct, largely so the jewellery doesn't draw attention away from the day, but there's no firm rule against colour, and wearing something specifically connected to the person who died is widely seen as personal, not inappropriate.

### Are cremation diamonds really made from ashes?

Partially, and the exact proportion varies significantly by provider. Cremated remains alone don't always contain enough recoverable carbon to grow a diamond of meaningful size, and some companies supplement with additional carbon without making this clear to customers. Ask any provider directly what proportion of the diamond's carbon comes from the remains you send, in writing, before paying a deposit.

### What's the cheapest way to memorialise someone in jewellery?

A birthstone or engraved piece, typically from around £50, since it requires no ashes, hair, or specialist processing. A simple fingerprint casting is the next step up in cost and offers a more physical connection to the person, usually still under £200.

### Can pet ashes be made into jewellery the same way?

Yes. The materials and process are chemically identical to human ashes jewellery, and most UK providers who make memorial jewellery from human remains offer the same services for pets.
