# The Georgian Poison Ring: History, Mystery, and Hidden Compartments

The note said the powder was for cleaning pebbles.

It was August 1751 in Henley-on-Thames, and the pebbles were Scotch pebbles, a polished cryptocrystalline quartz that Captain William Henry Cranstoun had sent south to Mary Blandy as gifts, [wrapped in packets of white arsenic](https://chiswickcalendar.co.uk/heirs-grievances-the-georgian-daughter-who-poisoned-her-father-for-love/). Two servants who ate the leftovers from the Blandy table had fallen ill over the summer. On 5 August they found a letter mentioning "powder to clean the pebbles" and fetched the family doctor. Her father, Francis, the town clerk, died on 14 August. Mary was tried at Oxford on 3 March 1752 and hanged on 6 April.

Nothing in that case involves a ring. The poison travelled in a paper packet and went into gruel. I begin here because it is a well-documented Georgian poisoning, and because it wrecks the scene everyone expects: a woman in silk turning the bezel of her ring over a glass of wine.

The Georgian poison ring is real as an object and shaky as a story. Rings with hinged bezels and hollow cavities survive from the 18th century, museums catalogue some of them under that name, and dealers sell them too. What they held is a separate question, and the evidence points somewhere gentler and sadder than murder.

> *In Brief: "Poison ring" is a label, and I could find no documented Georgian case that earns it. Georgian compartment rings (the era runs 1714 to 1837) were made for hair, portraits, scent and mourning. The Science Museum Group holds a gold ring catalogued as a poison ring, possibly Italian and possibly 18th century, beside a possibly 18th-century ring containing hair and a memento mori ring dated 1758. The ancient tales (Hannibal, the Borgias) are tradition or unproven, the Georgian-era death most often attached to a ring, Condorcet in 1794, has an uncertain cause, and a well-documented Georgian poisoning, Mary Blandy's, involved arsenic in a packet. Buy a compartment ring for what it is, and treat the word poison as marketing.*

## The ring in the Wellcome cabinet

The Science Museum Group's catalogue entry for [object A129174](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co155261/poison-ring) is short. It describes a gold poison ring with a circular receptacle under a hinged lid set with blue and red stones, possibly Italian, possibly 18th century. The measurements do the rest: ring diameter 22mm, receptacle diameter 20mm, receptacle depth 8mm. It came in with Sir Henry Wellcome's museum collection, and the museum lists it for the Making the Modern World gallery, with a request to contact the museum before you visit.

A little arithmetic, mine and not the museum's. A cylinder 20mm across and 8mm deep holds about 2.5 millilitres, roughly half a teaspoon, and that is a ceiling, because the walls and lid take up room. The catalogue does not say what the receptacle held, and I could find no record that anyone knows.

Two neighbouring records in the same collection make the point. [A28506](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co154862/memento-mori-finger-ring-with-setting-containing-human-hair) is a gold ring, possibly 18th century, 24mm across, with a transparent setting containing human hair, catalogued as a memento mori ring. [A641566](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co156349/memento-mori-finger-ring) is dated 1758, made of gold and pinchbeck, 22mm across, with a skull and crossbones under an oval faceted stone and an enamelled inscription on the band. Three rings, one basic idea, and only one of them wears the word poison. The label follows the legend.

I came to these rings for the poison, and I suspect most people do.

## Where the legend comes from

The stories are old, and none of them is Georgian. Tradition has it that Hannibal [ended his life in 183 BC with poison from a ring](https://www.thedailybeast.com/the-wild-history-of-poison-rings/). [GIA](https://4cs.gia.edu/en-us/blog/murky-history-poison-rings/) repeats Pliny the Elder's account of a Roman official who escaped torture by biting into his poison ring, which had a thin shell as its container, and notes that the teenage Emperor Heliogabalus wore one and was assassinated before he could use it.

The strongest physical evidence is medieval. Excavators at the Cape Kaliakra fortress in Bulgaria found a [14th-century ring with a hollow cartridge decorated with granulation and a hole made on purpose](https://kashgar.com.au/blogs/jewellery-stones/the-poison-ring). The head of the dig said the hole was placed so that the wearer's finger would cover it, and the ring was probably worn on the little finger of the right hand. The theory, reported by [GIA](https://4cs.gia.edu/en-us/blog/murky-history-poison-rings/), that the ruler Dobrotitsa (1347 to 1386) used it against a rival family remains a theory.

Then come the Borgias. Lucrezia Borgia (1480 to 1519) is the name everyone reaches for, and [GIA notes that she was said to be adept](https://4cs.gia.edu/en-us/blog/murky-history-poison-rings/) with such rings while pointing out that fitting a fatal, tasteless dose into one was extremely difficult. A jewellery-making blogger is blunter: [there is no evidence the Borgias ever used them](https://www.beadinggem.com/2007/01/legend-of-borgia-poison-rings.html). A 1909 note in the medical journal Hospital, archived by PubMed Central, says of Lucrezia's supposed poisonings that [there is practically no historical proof](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5199993/). The Hallmark Society, a collectors' site, treats the [Borgia branding as mostly a nineteenth-century addition](https://hallmarksociety.com/poison-rings/) that never stopped selling.

So the Georgian ring inherits its reputation from Roman anecdote, Renaissance rumour and Victorian romance. None of that tells you what a ring made in 1770 was for.

## What Georgian compartments held

Start with the date. The Georgian era is usually given as [1714 to 1837](https://antiquejewellers.com/collections/mourning-rings), from George I to the end of William IV. In those years a common reason to put a hidden space in a ring was death.

By the early 18th century, [British families routinely handed mourning rings to a broad circle of acquaintances](https://www.hhantiquejewelry.com/shop/1733-deaths-head-memento-mori-ring/), with as much regard for status as for grief. Georgian mourning rings [paired gold and enamel with hair beneath crystal or glass](https://antiquejewellers.com/collections/mourning-rings), and their inscriptions gave a name, a date and an age. Collectors prize the swivel or locket ring, whose bezel turns to show hairwork on the back. The hair itself was arranged into wheat sheaves, trees and plumes on wire armatures, a small piece of engineering built to hold a person.

The categories blurred. Memento mori rings, which meditate on mortality in general, and mourning rings, which commemorate a named person, [used the same refined techniques by the late 18th century](https://antiquejewellers.com/blogs/ring-styles/memento-mori-rings). The same dealer's guide draws the distinction that matters here: a [memento mori ring is defined by its iconography](https://antiquejewellers.com/collections/memento-mori-rings) and a poison ring by a hinged compartment, so the two categories are separate. For more on the skull-and-hourglass end of the family, see [How to Wear a Memento Mori](/edit/how-to-wear-a-memento-mori), and for how the Georgians and Victorians wore their dead, [the jewellery of grief](/edit/jewellery-of-grief).

The Hallmark Society's account of [what genuine compartment rings carried](https://hallmarksociety.com/poison-rings/) is devotional and sentimental: relics, hair, portraits, scent and pills. Scent rings were tiny cousins of the vinaigrette, with a sponge under a pierced or solid lid. Poisoning, it concludes, is better preserved in anecdote and fiction than in the surviving evidence.

## How Georgians actually poisoned each other

Return to Henley. Arsenic in the 18th century was a white powder that could be carried in paper and stirred into food, and it was. At Mary Blandy's trial, the family doctor Anthony Addington [heated a white powder from the bottom of a gruel pan and noted the garlicky smell of arsenic](https://www.sciencehistory.org/stories/magazine/prefiguring-the-arsenic-wars/). The trial is often [described as the first murder trial to use scientific evidence](https://georgepallas.com/blog/2020/08/mary-blandy/), and the test was crude by later standards: James Marsh's much more reliable method arrived in 1836.

Mary said she thought the powder was a love potion that would make her father accept Cranstoun. Few believed her. Cranstoun, who already had a wife in Scotland, [fled to France and died there in December 1752](https://georgepallas.com/blog/2020/08/mary-blandy/). The lesson for a ring collector is that the case has everything a poison ring story wants (a love affair, a fortune, a gem-adjacent gift) and no ring. The poisoner needed a packet and a pan.

The one Georgian-era death routinely attached to a ring is Condorcet's. The mathematician and philosopher was found dead in his cell at Bourg-la-Reine on 29 March 1794, two days after his arrest. [How he died is unknown](https://www.worldhistory.org/Marquis_de_Condorcet/). The most widely accepted theory is poison, taken to avoid a public execution, though some historians think he was murdered or died of exhaustion, and [the New World Encyclopedia](https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Marquis_de_Condorcet) says the theory is that his friend Cabanis supplied it. Popular poison-ring articles, such as [HandWiki's](https://handwiki.org/wiki/Chemistry:Poison_ring), count him as a victim of one. The biographical accounts I found do not mention a ring.

## Why the label survived

Because it sells. The Hallmark Society notes that the compartment ring [draws reproductions more than most genres](https://hallmarksociety.com/poison-rings/), precisely because the story is worth money, which is why checking the date matters. The Daily Beast's piece pictures a 19th-century American ring by Marcus & Co. that, it says, was [probably never meant to carry poison](https://www.thedailybeast.com/the-wild-history-of-poison-rings/), and yet it appears in the story.

Museums have their own reasons. A catalogue entry inherits the assumptions of whoever first described the object, and the Science Museum Group says plainly that it [cannot guarantee the accuracy of its records](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co155261/poison-ring). I would not take a museum label as evidence of use, and I would not take it as evidence against either.

## Seeing one, and buying one

To see a Georgian compartment ring, contact the [Science Museum](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co155261/poison-ring) before you go, because the objects are listed for a gallery and display changes. To buy one, [Grays](https://www.visitlondon.com/things-to-do/place/160293-grays-antique-market) in Mayfair has nearly 100 specialist dealers and is listed as open Monday to Friday, and [The Antique Jewellery Company](https://www.antiquejewellerycompany.com/) lists lockets among its categories and sees clients at a Mayfair townhouse by appointment.

Whoever you buy from, ask what the compartment holds now. Hair under crystal points to a mourning or sentimental ring, and residue is a matter for a specialist. Ask who dated the ring as Georgian, and on what evidence. Ask whether the hinge and catch are original. If the only reason offered for calling it a poison ring is the Borgias, you are being sold a story, and stories are cheaper than rings. For the care of enamel, hair and old gold once you own one, [our care guide](/guides/how-to-care-for-your-jewellery) covers what shortens a piece's life.

## The honest name

Call it a compartment ring. It is the better story: a piece of Georgian engineering built to hold a lock of hair, a name and a date, worn on the hand of someone who could not bear to put the dead down. The poison belongs in the packets where the court found it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Were poison rings real?

Rings with hidden compartments are real, and some ancient and medieval examples, such as the 14th-century ring from Cape Kaliakra in Bulgaria, have features that suggest a deliberate opening for a substance. For Georgian Britain I found no documented case of a murder or suicide committed with one, and most surviving Georgian compartment rings are locket or mourning rings.

### Did Lucrezia Borgia use a poison ring?

There is no evidence that she did. GIA says she was said to be adept with such rings, a jewellery-making blog says there is no evidence the Borgias ever used them, and a 1909 medical-journal note archived by PubMed Central says there is practically no historical proof of the poisonings attributed to her.

### What did Georgian compartment rings hold?

Hair, most often. Georgian mourning rings and swivel or locket rings carried hairwork and painted miniatures, and other compartment rings held relics, scent on a sponge, or pills, according to the Hallmark Society.

### Is a memento mori ring the same as a poison ring?

No. A memento mori ring is defined by mortality iconography such as skulls and hourglasses, while a poison ring is defined by a hinged compartment. The two can overlap, as with the Science Museum's 1758 ring, which holds a skull and crossbones under a faceted stone, but the compartment is not evidence of poison.

### How can I tell whether a compartment ring is really Georgian?

Not from the compartment alone. Reproductions of the genre are common, so ask the seller who dated it and on what evidence, what the compartment contains, and whether the hinge and catch are original, and have an independent gemmologist or auction specialist look at anything expensive.

### Where can I see a Georgian poison ring?

The Science Museum Group holds a gold ring catalogued as a poison ring, possibly Italian and possibly 18th century, listed for its Making the Modern World gallery. Contact the museum before you visit to check that it is on display.
