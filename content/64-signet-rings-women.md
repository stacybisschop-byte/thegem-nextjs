# Signet Rings for Women: The Complete Buying Guide

Henrietta Maria, queen consort to Charles I, owned a signet ring set with a diamond engraved with the royal arms. The stone was cut as a lozenge, not the shield shape her husband's arms would have used, because English heraldic convention placed a man's coat of arms in a shield and a woman's in a diamond. The distinction wasn't decorative. It was the formal grammar of the period, and it tells you something most "signet rings for women" content skips entirely: the ring itself was never gendered. The shield was. Arms passed down the male line in Britain for centuries, which meant the crest-and-signet tradition became coded male by inheritance, not because a woman wearing one was doing anything unconventional.

That history matters now for a practical reason. Search "signet ring for women" today and you'll land in one of two distinct markets without much signal about which one you're in. Hancocks, the Bond Street house that's made signets since 1849, states plainly that its rings are "worn by both men and women" and that it "designs its range for all wearers," with no separate women's line. [Astley Clarke](https://www.astleyclarke.com/us/jewellery/rings/signet-rings/signet-rings-for-women), by contrast, runs a dedicated collection called Signet Rings for Women, sized from J to P specifically for smaller hands, cast instead of hand-engraved, and priced for a fashion purchase, not an heirloom. Both are legitimately signet rings. They are not the same product, and knowing which one you actually want will save you a wasted conversation with the wrong maker.

> *In Brief: Two distinct markets both sell signet rings to women. Heritage houses (Rebus, Hancocks, Deakin & Francis, Bentley & Skinner) are technically ungendered, hand-engraved, made to order in solid gold, and built as heirlooms; sizing runs to whatever face suits your hand, not a "women's" range. Contemporary demi-fine houses (Astley Clarke, Monica Vinader) run dedicated women's collections, cast instead of engraved, sized smaller as standard, and priced as a fashion purchase, not a lifetime one. Neither is more "correct." They serve different intentions.*

## The heritage market: technically ungendered, historically male-coded

[The signet ring edit](/edit/signet-ring-edit) covers the makers, engraving choices, and materials in this category in full, so this isn't a repeat of that ground. What's worth adding here specifically is the gender question, because it's more settled than the marketing imagery suggests.

Hancocks, Rebus, and Deakin & Francis all sell into a single range regardless of who's buying: the same shapes, the same face sizes, the same engraving options. A woman ordering a bespoke crest signet from any of these houses is not buying from a smaller or lesser tier. She's buying the identical product a man would order, sized to her hand instead of a gendered default. The convention that made these rings read as masculine was the inheritance of arms, not the object. If you have your own entitlement to a crest, or want a monogram, initial, or motif in the same hand-engraved seal tradition, this is the market to be in, and the gender framing shouldn't factor into the decision at all.

What it costs you in exchange for that authenticity: time, and money. Bespoke hand engraving runs six to eight weeks at most heritage makers, longer for platinum or hardstone, and prices start around £400 to £600 in 9ct or 18ct gold, moving up sharply with crest complexity or hardstone setting.

## The contemporary market: built for women specifically, and built differently

This is the newer, and in most search results the more visible, category.

[Astley Clarke's](https://www.astleyclarke.com/us/jewellery/rings/signet-rings/signet-rings-for-women) Signet Rings for Women collection is sized J to P, roughly two to three sizes smaller across the range than a typical heritage maker's default run, and designed around stacking instead of solitary wear: a chunky mother-of-pearl signet, a mini celestial orbit design, single-character engraving in a choice of fonts instead of a full crest or monogram. [Monica Vinader's](https://www.monicavinader.com/us/shop/signet-rings) Signature Signet Ring is smaller still (a 1cm square face, 2mm band width in the vermeil version) and offered in sterling silver or 18ct gold vermeil, not solid gold, with free engraving and free resizing included as standard.

Both are cast, not hand seal-engraved, which is a real difference in how the piece is made, not a lesser version of the same process. Casting a design is faster and cheaper than cutting it by hand into a solid face, and it means the ring can carry a single initial or a small motif instead of the depth a hand-engraved crest needs. Prices reflect that: Monica Vinader and Astley Clarke both sit in the £75 to £250 range, a fraction of a bespoke heritage piece, and both design explicitly for stacking, since neither brand expects the signet to be worn alone.

## Which market you actually want

**Buying an heirloom, have a crest or want a serious monogram, plan to pass it on:** the heritage makers. [The signet ring edit](/edit/signet-ring-edit) has the full maker comparison.

**Building a stack, want a single initial or a motif instead of a crest, buying for now instead of for a lifetime:** the contemporary houses. Astley Clarke if you want the widest range of stone-set and statement options; Monica Vinader if you want the smallest, most minimal face and free resizing included.

**Undecided:** the honest tiebreaker is whether you'd be upset to lose it. A £150 vermeil signet lost on a night out is an annoyance. A £1,200 hand-engraved 18ct piece is a different category of loss, and that alone pushes a lot of first-time buyers toward the contemporary market before they're ready to commit to the heritage one.

## Wearing one alongside an engagement ring

This is the question the heritage-market content rarely addresses, because it was written assuming a male buyer.

The simplest solution is the right hand: a signet worn on the right ring finger or right pinky sits with no engagement or wedding convention to navigate at all, which is why it's become the default recommendation regardless of gender. If you'd rather keep it on the left hand alongside an engagement ring, proportion is what matters: a slim contemporary signet can sit quietly next to a solitaire without competing for attention, while a substantial heritage piece in the same position usually reads as too much happening on one hand. [Our guide to engagement ring stones](/guides/engagement-ring-stones) covers how a solitaire's setting affects what can comfortably sit beside it, which is worth reading alongside this if you're planning the combination in advance instead of working it out after the fact.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can women wear signet rings?

Yes, without qualification, and have throughout history. Signet rings were never gendered as objects; the crest-and-arms tradition became associated with men because titles and coats of arms passed down the male line in Britain. Women of rank who held their own entitlement to arms, such as Henrietta Maria, wore signets set in the lozenge shape that heraldic convention reserved for women's arms, as distinct from the shield used for men's.

### What's the difference between a women's signet ring and a regular one?

At heritage makers like Hancocks and Rebus, there is no difference; the range is designed for all wearers and sized to the individual hand. At contemporary demi-fine houses like Astley Clarke and Monica Vinader, "women's signet ring" refers to a real, distinct product: smaller as standard, cast instead of hand-engraved, usually in sterling silver or gold vermeil instead of solid gold, and designed for stacking, not solitary wear.

### What finger should a woman wear a signet ring on?

There's no rule specific to women; the general signet convention applies regardless of gender. Traditionally the little finger of the non-dominant hand, though the right-hand ring finger has become the most common contemporary choice, largely because it carries no engagement or wedding ring convention to navigate.

### Can you wear a signet ring with an engagement ring?

Yes, and it's increasingly common on the same hand as well as the opposite one. The right hand avoids the question entirely. On the left hand alongside an engagement ring, a slimmer, quieter signet sits more comfortably than a substantial heritage piece, which tends to compete with a solitaire for visual weight.

### Are contemporary signet rings lower quality than heritage ones?

Not lower quality; differently made for a different purpose. Casting is a legitimate, widely used jewellery-making technique, and houses like Astley Clarke and Monica Vinader use it deliberately to offer a smaller, lighter, more affordable piece designed for stacking. It isn't a cheaper attempt at what heritage makers do; it's a different product built to a different brief, in the same way a demi-fine chain and a solid gold chain both serve real, distinct purposes.
