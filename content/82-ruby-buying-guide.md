# Ruby Buying Guide: The Most Misunderstood Stone in Fine Jewellery

Ruby and sapphire are the same mineral, corundum, and the only difference between them is which trace element got into the crystal while it was forming. Chromium makes it red, and red corundum is ruby; almost anything else makes it a sapphire. That single fact explains more about ruby's reputation than any amount of marketing copy: it is chemically the rarer, more difficult colour, and the trade has spent two centuries pricing it accordingly. It has also spent the last twenty years selling a category of treated material so far from natural ruby that selling it simply as ruby is misleading.

> *In Brief: Ruby quality comes down to colour first (hue, tone, saturation, with "pigeon blood" as the trade's benchmark description, not a universal grade), then clarity, cut, and carat. Origin adds real prestige: Burma's Mogok Valley set the standard, Mozambique is now the practical source of fine colour, and both the US and UK maintain sanctions against Myanmar's state-owned gem enterprise. Heat treatment is normal and expected in the vast majority of rubies on the market; lead-glass filling is a different, heavily disguised category that should cost a fraction of a genuine heated stone. Expect roughly £100–£900 per carat for commercial to good heated red, and considerably more for fine colour or certified Burmese and Mozambican origin.*

## Colour comes first, and "pigeon blood" is the whole argument

Ruby is graded on the same three components as any coloured stone, and here they matter more than almost anywhere else in the trade.

**Hue** is the base colour. Ruby's accepted range runs from a slightly orangey red through to a slightly purplish red, and the finest hue sits close to a pure red with the faintest blue undertone, no orange and no brown creeping in. Stones with too much pink read as pink sapphire instead, a distinction that has real pricing consequences and no fixed boundary; GIA's principle is that red must be the dominant hue before a stone can be called ruby, and labs judge it stone by stone.

**Tone** runs light to dark, and the sweet spot for ruby is medium to medium-dark. Too pale and the stone reads as pink; too dark and it goes toward garnet, losing the brilliance that makes ruby worth owning.

**Saturation** is where the value concentrates. A highly saturated medium-toned red will consistently outprice a paler, larger stone, which is the ruby version of the mistake sapphire buyers make with carat weight: chasing size over colour intensity is the single easiest way to overpay.

"Pigeon blood" is the industry's shorthand for the top of this range, first used in 1829 to describe the finest rubies coming out of Mogok: a vivid, pure red with a faint blue undertone and strong red fluorescence that gives the stone an internal glow. It is a colour designation that a handful of specialist labs, notably SSEF, Gübelin and GRS, apply to stones meeting their own criteria (SSEF formalised its standard in 1996), not a universal grade with a single fixed formula, and it is used loosely by sellers who know what those two words are worth. A stone can be marketed as pigeon blood and still miss the tone and saturation a serious lab would require. Treat the phrase as a claim to be checked against a report, never as a reason to pay more on its own.

## Clarity works differently here than it does for diamonds

Like sapphire, ruby is a "Type II" stone in the clarity classifications gemmologists use for coloured gems: inclusions are expected, and an eye-clean example is the exception, not the rule, especially above a carat or two. GIA puts it plainly: inclusion-free rubies are practically nonexistent.

Some of those inclusions are desirable. Fine rutile needles, known as silk, contribute to the soft glow that the best Mogok stones are known for, in the same way silk does for Kashmir sapphire, and their presence in moderation is not a defect. Intact silk is also a useful clue that a stone hasn't been heated at very high temperatures.

Heavily included ruby is where the real risk sits, because material too fractured to sell as anything else is exactly what gets treated with lead glass, covered in the treatment section below. An unusually clean, bright ruby at a low price is the first thing to be suspicious of, not the last.

## Cut affects colour more than people expect

A well-cut ruby returns even colour across the face of the stone; a poorly cut one shows extinction, dark patches where light isn't returning, or windowing where the centre looks washed out. Ruby rough tends to form in shapes that favour ovals and cushions, which is why those two cuts dominate fine ruby jewellery; round rubies exist but demand cutters sacrifice more of the rough to achieve them, and cost more for it. Prioritise a stone that glows evenly over one cut purely to save weight. A well-cut good stone will consistently outperform a poorly cut exceptional one when you're actually wearing it.

## Where a ruby is from still matters, sometimes enormously

Origin changes what collectors will pay, and for ruby the premium at the top is some of the steepest in the coloured-stone trade.

**Burma (Mogok)** is the origin every serious buyer eventually has to reckon with. The Mogok Valley, Myanmar's "Valley of Rubies," is where the finest rubies form in marble deposits low in iron, which is part of why the best examples combine strong colour with unusually bright fluorescence. The US banned Burmese rubies under the 2008 JADE Act, lifted the ban in 2016, and after the 2021 coup sanctioned Myanmar's state-owned gem enterprise; the UK sanctioned the same enterprise in 2021. So ask specifically how any Burmese stone reached the market and whether the paperwork accounts for it; a specialist dealer or auction house should be able to answer this without hesitation.

**Mozambique** has become the practical centre of the fine-ruby market since the early 2010s, producing vivid, well-saturated stones that rival Burmese colour at meaningfully lower prices. For most buyers today, this is where fine natural ruby actually gets bought.

**Sri Lanka (Ceylon)** produces high-clarity stones that generally sit at the pinker, brighter end of ruby's accepted range, lighter than the deep pigeon blood red, still genuinely ruby but a different personality.

**Thailand** was historically an important source and remains a significant cutting and trading centre, though its own mined material tends toward darker, more purplish stones that command less than Burmese or Mozambican colour.

## Treatment: what's normal, and what to actually worry about

Heat treatment is standard for ruby, in the same way it is for sapphire: it can remove unwanted purplish or bluish tints and improve clarity, it is permanent and stable, and it is disclosed on any reputable lab report. Assume any ruby you're shown has been heated unless a certificate states otherwise. A heat-treated ruby with a proper report is a legitimate, fairly priced gemstone, and unheated stones at fine colour grades carry a real, substantial premium precisely because they are rare.

Lead-glass filling is a different matter entirely, and it is the treatment that defines the bottom of today's ruby market. The process takes low-grade, heavily fractured material and fills the cracks with a lead-rich glass whose refractive index closely matches corundum, disguising fractures that would otherwise make the stone unsellable. The glass can account for a large share of the stone's total volume. The major labs describe heavily filled material as a "ruby-glass composite", and GIA's reports call the most extreme cases "a manufactured product"; trade bodies including AGTA and the ICA require the treatment to be disclosed at every point of sale, so it cannot be passed off as a treated natural stone. The value gap is not subtle: a glass-filled stone might retail for a few pounds a carat, against hundreds or thousands for a natural heated ruby of similar apparent colour and clarity. It is also fragile in ways that matter day to day, since a jeweller's torch, ultrasonic or steam cleaners, and household acids and chemicals can damage the fill and visibly harm the stone. If a "ruby" looks unusually clean and bright for its price, glass filling is the most likely explanation, and it should be disclosed on the receipt in those words.

## Certification: when to ask, and who to ask

As with sapphire, GIA's reports on ruby confirm natural origin, disclose detectable treatment, and can offer a geographic origin opinion on request; for anything above a token sum, that report is not optional. Alongside GIA, the labs most trusted in the coloured-stone trade for ruby are SSEF, Gübelin and GRS, particularly where Burmese or Mozambican origin is being claimed on a significant stone.

Ask directly: is this heated, and how was that determined? Has it been lead-glass filled, and if so how does the report describe it? What is the declared origin, and is there paperwork to support both the gemmology and, for Burmese material, its legal route to market? A seller who answers plainly is doing their job properly. A seller who calls a clean, cheap red stone simply "ruby" with no further detail is not.

## FAQ

**Is heat-treated ruby worth buying?** Yes. The large majority of rubies on the market are heat-treated, and it's a permanent, stable, fully accepted practice that improves colour and clarity without disguising the stone's structure. Unheated stones carry a real premium at fine colour grades, but heat treatment alone is not a reason to avoid a ruby.

**What is a glass-filled or composite ruby?** It's heavily fractured, low-grade corundum that has had its cracks filled with lead-rich glass to disguise them and improve apparent clarity and colour. The glass can make up a large share of the stone by volume, labs describe it as a ruby-glass composite (GIA calls the most extreme cases a manufactured product), and it is priced at a small fraction of a genuine heated natural ruby of similar appearance.

**Why is Burmese ruby so expensive?** Mogok has produced the trade's benchmark colour for two centuries, the deposits are limited, and both the US and UK maintain sanctions against Myanmar's state-owned gem enterprise, which constrains and complicates legitimate supply further. Reputation, scarcity and legal friction all push the price up together.

**What does "pigeon blood" actually mean?** It's a trade description for the finest ruby colour: a vivid, pure red with a faint blue undertone and strong fluorescence, historically associated with the best Mogok stones. It is assigned by specialist gemmological labs such as SSEF, Gübelin and GRS against their own criteria, not a single universal grade, and sellers use the phrase far more often than labs actually confirm it.

**Where should I actually buy a ruby?** For set jewellery, look for a retailer that's a member of the [National Association of Jewellers](https://www.naj.co.uk/) (NAJ), whose members agree to a code of conduct on honest disclosure. For loose or investment-grade stones, buy from a specialist coloured-stone dealer who supplies an independent lab report as standard, not an in-house certificate; the [Gemmological Association of Great Britain](https://gem-a.com) is a good starting point for understanding what proper credentials look like. For Burmese or fine Mozambican origin, or anything at significant carat weight, the major auction houses handle provenance-documented stones regularly: [Sotheby's](https://www.sothebys.com/en/buy/luxury/jewelry) and [Bonhams](https://www.bonhams.com/department/JWL/jewellery/) both run dedicated jewellery departments online, alongside Christie's.
