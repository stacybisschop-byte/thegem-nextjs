# Jackie Kennedy's Jewellery: An Education in Restraint

In April 1996, Sotheby's New York held an auction of Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis's personal effects, two years after her death. One of the most contested lots was not a diamond or an emerald. It was a triple-strand necklace of simulated pearls, designed by the American costume jeweller Kenneth Jay Lane, that had cost perhaps a few hundred dollars new. It sold for $211,500, bought by the Franklin Mint for a private museum.

That price tells you almost everything about how Jackie Kennedy actually used jewellery, and almost nothing about what her jewellery was actually worth. She owned real stones of genuine consequence: a Van Cleef & Arpels engagement ring, a forty-carat diamond from Harry Winston. She wore fakes of her own real jewels more often than the originals, on purpose, and the fakes are what people remember. Understanding why requires taking her seriously as a strategist, not simply as a style icon, which is usually as far as this story gets told.

> *In Brief: Jackie Kennedy owned two significant engagement rings — a Van Cleef & Arpels toi et moi from John F. Kennedy in 1953, and a 40.42-carat Harry Winston diamond from Aristotle Onassis in 1968 — but the piece she's remembered for is a costume pearl necklace by Kenneth Jay Lane that cost a fraction of either. She commissioned Lane to copy her finest real jewellery so she could wear the replicas in public and keep the originals secured. The strategy, not any single piece, is the actual story.*

## The first ring

John F. Kennedy proposed to Jacqueline Bouvier in June 1953 with a Van Cleef & Arpels toi et moi ring: a 2.88-carat emerald-cut diamond set beside a 2.84-carat emerald-cut emerald, two stones of near-identical weight sitting side by side instead of one stone dominating the setting. The ring was bought at Van Cleef's Fifth Avenue boutique, reportedly selected by Joseph Kennedy, JFK's father, with the help of Hélène Arpels. The unconventional two-stone format broke from the standard solitaire and set a template other public engagements have followed since.

In 1962, with Kennedy in the White House, the ring went back to Van Cleef & Arpels for a reset: marquise and round-cut diamonds added around the original centre stones, a more elaborate setting built to match her position as First Lady. The updated ring, not the original, is what survives today at the John F. Kennedy Presidential Library and Museum in Boston.

## The second ring

Aristotle Onassis proposed in 1968 with something considerably less restrained: a 40.42-carat marquise-cut diamond, the Lesotho III, cut by Harry Winston from a 601-carat rough that had been discovered in Lesotho the previous year and cleaved on live television. It was, at the time, one of the largest gem-quality diamonds in circulation, and by any measure a statement ring.

Jackie rarely wore it. Accounts from the period describe her keeping it in a bank vault instead of on her hand, bringing it out for the smallest number of occasions a piece that size could plausibly justify. It sold at the same 1996 Sotheby's sale as the pearls, for $2,587,500, a fraction of what any comparable stone might command today but a clear multiple of its pre-sale estimate, driven by the same thing that drove the pearls: her name attached to the object.

## The pearls, and the actual strategy

The triple-strand faux pearl necklace, designed for her by Kenneth Jay Lane and worn constantly through the White House years, tucked into day suits and evening gowns alike, was not an isolated eccentricity. It was one piece in a working system.

[Lane](https://kennethjaylane.com), whose client list also included the Duchess of Windsor and Elizabeth Taylor, was commissioned by Jackie specifically to reproduce her significant real pieces, including a Van Cleef & Arpels necklace given to her by Onassis, in exchange for the right to sell his own versions commercially. The arrangement let her wear convincing replicas in public, in front of photographers, at events where a piece could be lost, damaged, or simply draw more attention than she wanted, while the originals stayed secured. It's the same instinct that led [Wallis Simpson](/stories/wallis-simpson-jewellery) toward a handful of extraordinary, deliberately deployed pieces instead of a visible everyday collection, arrived at from the opposite direction: Wallis wore the real thing sparingly for maximum effect; Jackie wore convincing fakes constantly so the real thing never had to leave the vault.

Barbara Bush had a copy of the same pearl necklace made and wore it to her husband's 1989 inauguration, a direct, acknowledged homage that shows how completely the piece had become shorthand for a certain kind of American public elegance, independent of what it was actually made of.

## What the restraint was actually doing

Jackie Kennedy had access to jewellery of real consequence and chose, most of the time, not to wear it. That choice did real work. A triple-strand pearl necklace photographs cleanly in black and white, reads at a distance, and doesn't compete with what she was wearing or what she was doing. It let the pearls become a uniform, not an event, something she could put on daily without the necklace itself becoming the story each time. The forty-carat diamond, by contrast, could only ever be an event, which is precisely why it stayed in the vault.

The comparison worth making is with [Elizabeth Taylor](/stories/elizabeth-taylor-jewellery), who also wore Kenneth Jay Lane on occasion, but whose collection was built around the opposite premise: pieces meant to be seen, discussed, and photographed as the main event. Taylor's jewellery was the performance. Jackie's restraint was the performance, and the real stones were what she kept out of frame.

## The 1996 sale

The scale of the 1996 Sotheby's auction, and the prices individual lots achieved, mirror something that happened almost a decade earlier at the [Sotheby's Geneva sale of Wallis Simpson's collection](/stories/wallis-simpson-jewellery): both sales made clear that a famous woman's jewellery, once it left her hands, could be worth a multiple of its material value purely for having belonged to her. The difference is in which objects carried that premium. At the Windsor sale, it was extraordinary real jewels: a Cartier flamingo, a Tutti Frutti necklace. At the Kennedy Onassis sale, a fake pearl necklace outsold plenty of the real stones in the same catalogue, on name alone. ## Frequently asked questions

### Were Jackie Kennedy's pearls real?

Her most famous necklace, the triple-strand piece she wore constantly during the White House years, was costume jewellery designed by Kenneth Jay Lane, not genuine pearls. She did own real pearls and other fine jewellery, but the piece she's most associated with was, deliberately, a fake.

### What happened to Jackie Kennedy's jewellery?

Much of it was sold at a Sotheby's auction in April 1996, two years after her death, as part of the sale of her personal estate. Individual pieces, including the Kenneth Jay Lane faux pearl necklace and the 40.42-carat Lesotho III diamond from Aristotle Onassis, sold for prices well above their material value, reflecting the premium attached to her ownership. Her original 1953 Van Cleef & Arpels engagement ring, as later reset, is held at the John F. Kennedy Presidential Library and Museum.

### How much was Jackie Kennedy's engagement ring worth?

She had two. The 1953 Van Cleef & Arpels toi et moi ring from John F. Kennedy, featuring a 2.88-carat diamond and a 2.84-carat emerald, was reportedly worth around $1 million at the time of purchase. The 1968 ring from Aristotle Onassis, a 40.42-carat Harry Winston diamond known as the Lesotho III, sold at auction in 1996 for $2,587,500.

### Why did Jackie Kennedy wear fake jewellery?

She commissioned the costume jeweller Kenneth Jay Lane to reproduce several of her significant real pieces so she could wear convincing replicas in public while keeping the originals secured, instead of exposing valuable jewellery to the risks of constant public appearances. The strategy meant her most photographed pieces were often fakes, while the real versions stayed out of sight.
