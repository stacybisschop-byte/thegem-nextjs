# The Best Tennis Bracelets to Buy in the US: A US Open Edit

In 1978, a diamond bracelet slid off Chris Evert's wrist mid-match at the US Open, and play stopped while she went looking for it on the court. [The full story is worth reading on its own](/stories/tennis-bracelet-history), but the short version is that the category didn't have a name before that afternoon; it did afterward. Fifty years on, the piece that made her famous for something other than tennis has become one of the most consistently worn styles in fine jewellery, and the US Open remains the moment it gets talked about most.

If you're buying one before this year's tournament, here's where to actually find one, at every price point, shipped and ready before Fan Week starts.

> *In Brief: A tennis bracelet is a flexible line of individually set stones, traditionally diamond, in a continuous symmetrical pattern. Moissanite versions start around $300 to $600; lab-grown diamond from roughly $800; natural diamond from around $1,800 for a solid one-carat piece; fine jewellery houses like Tiffany move into five figures. Whatever the budget, check for a secondary safety clasp specifically, since the reason the category exists at all is that Chris Evert's original didn't have one.*

## What actually makes a tennis bracelet

A tennis bracelet is a continuous line of individually set stones, typically diamonds, in a flexible metal setting, symmetrical along its full length with no single stone acting as a centrepiece. The two common ways to set the stones are prong and bezel: three or four metal prongs holding each stone with light passing through from all sides, or a bezel that wraps a thin rim of metal fully around each stone's edge. Both are secure. Bezel settings sit lower against the skin and catch less on clothing, which makes them a better choice for daily wear, not just a style preference, while prong settings let slightly more light into the stone.

The clasp is the part of this history that actually matters. Evert's original bracelet had a single clasp and nothing backing it up; when it failed, there was nothing to stop the whole piece falling away. Reputable tennis bracelets today are built with a safety clasp as standard, usually a secondary figure-eight safety catch or a fine safety chain that holds the bracelet even if the main clasp opens. This isn't a premium feature reserved for expensive pieces. Check for it regardless of budget, and treat its absence as a reason to keep looking, not a minor omission.

## Under $800: moissanite

A moissanite tennis bracelet gets you the same continuous, flexible line of stones at a fraction of the cost of diamond, since moissanite runs roughly 5 to 15 percent of an equivalent diamond's price per stone. [Our full comparison of diamond and moissanite](/guides/diamond-vs-moissanite) covers the real trade-offs, including moissanite's noticeably higher fire and its considerably weaker resale value, both worth knowing before you buy a piece with dozens of stones, not just one.

This is where a genuine diamond tennis bracelet becomes realistic without a five-figure budget. [Brilliant Earth's](https://www.brilliantearth.com) Petite Lab Diamond Tennis Bracelet, at 1.5 carats total weight in a low-profile 14k gold setting, is built specifically for daily wear, and the brand's own buying guidance recommends exactly this kind of low-profile, secure setting for a piece you intend to wear constantly, not just for occasions. [James Allen's](https://www.jamesallen.com) 14k rose gold tennis bracelet, with diamonds set in four-prong baskets running the full length of the piece including up to the clasp itself, retails at $1,813, a useful real-world anchor for what a solid one-carat natural diamond piece costs at a reputable retailer. Blue Nile's 1-carat total weight natural diamond tennis bracelet sits in comparable territory. ## $5,000 and above: the fine jewellery tier

[Tiffany & Co.'s](https://www.tiffany.com) Victoria collection is the reference point here: graduated diamonds instead of uniform ones, each stone individually selected, in settings built to last generations, not seasons. [Cartier](https://www.cartier.com) holds comparable territory. Both houses price well into five figures for a substantial piece, and both are worth visiting in person if you're at this level, since the difference between a good tennis bracelet and an exceptional one becomes clearly visible at this quality of stone and setting.

## Getting the size right

Standard lengths run 6.5 to 7.5 inches, with petite and extended options available from most retailers. Measure your wrist and add roughly half an inch for a comfortable fit that still sits close instead of sliding around; too loose defeats the point of a design meant to sit flush against the skin. If you're between standard sizes, most retailers, including James Allen, offer a shortening service, so it's worth asking instead of assuming you have to round up or down.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between a tennis bracelet and a regular diamond bracelet?

A tennis bracelet specifically refers to a continuous line of individually set, uniformly sized stones running the full length of the piece, with no single stone or section acting as a focal point. Other diamond bracelets may use graduated stones, clusters, or a single centre stone in a way a true tennis bracelet does not.

### Why is it called a tennis bracelet?

Because of Chris Evert. During the 1978 US Open, her diamond bracelet, then simply called an in-line or eternity bracelet, came loose and fell off mid-match. Play paused while she looked for it, and the name stuck to the style afterward. [The full story](/stories/tennis-bracelet-history) covers what happened to the bracelet itself.

### Is lab-grown diamond a good choice for a tennis bracelet?

Yes, and arguably a better one than for a single-stone piece like an engagement ring, since a tennis bracelet's value lies mostly in the total visual effect of dozens of stones, not the pedigree of any single one. Lab-grown diamond offers identical brilliance and durability to natural diamond at a meaningfully lower price, which matters more when you're paying for twenty or thirty stones, not one.

### What should I check before buying a tennis bracelet?

A secondary safety clasp is the single most important feature to check, regardless of budget, given the style's own history. Beyond that, check whether the setting is prong or bezel (bezel sits lower and catches less for daily wear), confirm the total carat weight instead of relying on visual size alone, and ask whether a shortening or resizing service is included if you're not certain of the exact length you need.

### How do you clean a tennis bracelet?

Regular cleaning with a mild soap and warm water solution, using a soft brush to reach between and behind the stones, is sufficient for most tennis bracelets. An ultrasonic cleaner, either a jeweller's or a home unit, is effective for a deeper clean a few times a year. Avoid harsh chemicals and remove the bracelet before swimming or heavy exercise, since prolonged contact with chlorine and sweat can affect both the metal and any plating over time.
