# The Best Gold Hoop Earrings at Every Price Point

A hoop earring is the one piece of jewellery almost nobody argues about. Ask ten people whether they'd wear a signet ring or a statement necklace and you'll get ten different answers. Ask about hoops and the answer is almost always yes, in some size, in some weight, worn most days instead of saved for occasions. That universality is exactly why the category is worth more care than it usually gets. A hoop sits directly against the ear, in constant skin contact, worn longer per week than almost anything else in a jewellery box, and it's also the format most readily faked, because a hoop is a hoop: the silhouette does most of the aesthetic work, so a £5 pair and a £500 pair can look nearly identical in a photograph. The difference only shows up in the hand, on the ear, and after a year of actual wear.

What follows is organised by what your money is actually buying at each level, not just by price for its own sake.

> *In Brief: Below £50 buys gold-plated brass, fine for occasional wear but not for daily use against skin. £50 to £150 is gold vermeil territory (a thick layer of real gold over sterling silver), the honest entry point for regular wear. £150 to £400 moves into solid gold at the smaller, lighter end. Above £400, you're paying for solid 18ct gold, often with diamonds, built to last decades, not seasons. [Our gold guide](/guides/9ct-vs-18ct-gold) covers what the carat numbers themselves mean.*

## What actually changes between tiers

Three things separate a £15 hoop from a £400 one, and it's worth knowing what they are before you start comparing prices, because none of them are visible in a product photo.

**The metal.** Gold-plated brass is a thin layer of gold, sometimes only a few microns, over a base metal that will show through with wear. Gold vermeil is a thicker, regulated layer of real gold (in the UK, legally at least 2.5 microns) over sterling silver, not brass, which means better colour retention and no base-metal reaction against skin. Solid gold is gold all the way through, at whatever carat is stated, and it's the only one of the three with no plating to eventually wear off.

**The closure.** Small to medium hoops typically use a hinged snap closure, a small mechanism that clicks shut and holds the hoop's shape under normal wear. Larger or heavier hoops often move to a click-top or latch closure, which needs more precise engineering to stay secure at that weight. A loose or poorly fitted closure is one of the most common reasons a hoop goes missing, and it's rarely mentioned in marketing copy.

**The construction.** Hoops are made either as a solid tube of metal or a hollow one. Hollow tube construction is how larger statement hoops stay wearable at all, since a solid tube at that diameter would be too heavy for most ears. Smaller hoops can go either way; a solid small hoop feels more substantial and typically wears better over years, at the cost of some extra weight.

For a fuller explanation of what carat actually measures and why 9ct wears differently to 18ct, [our gold guide](/guides/9ct-vs-18ct-gold) covers the metallurgy in depth.

## Under £50: two different things wearing the same label

This tier isn't one product. It splits into gold-plated brass and something that isn't gold at all, and the two get marketed almost identically.

Topshop's Enya pearl hoop earrings, at £8.40, are stated plainly on their own product listing as zinc and acrylic with a "gold-tone" finish, which is cosmetic colour, not plating, let alone gold. Accessorize's celestial charm hoops, by contrast, are genuine gold plating over a brass base, sold at a similar price point. Both will look identical in a product photo. Only one of them has any actual gold content, and the listing rarely makes the distinction obvious unless you read the materials line closely.

Missoma, whose statement pieces use gold-plated brass with an anti-tarnish finish, states plainly on its own site that its plated and silver jewellery shouldn't go in the shower or the pool, since water contact accelerates plating wear. That's true of gold plating generally, at any brand, at this price, and it's worth taking as a general rule for the whole tier: buy here for a hoop you'll wear occasionally and retire without regret, or as a low-stakes way to try a size or shape before committing to it in a better metal. Don't expect a plated pair, and certainly not a zinc-and-acrylic pair, to still look new after a year of daily wear.

## £50–150: the honest entry point

This is where gold vermeil starts, and it's the tier worth thinking of as the real starting point for a hoop you intend to wear most days. Astley Clarke's small and medium Celestial Linia hoops in 18ct gold vermeil retail at £75 and £95 respectively, with a click closure the brand's own customer reviews single out specifically for feeling secure. Mejuri's Tube Huggie Hoops in 18k gold vermeil sit at $98 (roughly £77), and, more usefully, its Small Hoops at the same $98 price are 10k solid gold, not vermeil, which is worth knowing: at Mejuri specifically, solid gold and vermeil sometimes cost the same, and the material line on the product page is the only way to tell which you're getting.

Monica Vinader's hoops in 18ct gold vermeil over recycled sterling silver sit at the top of this range (its Siren Muse medium hoops retail around £150), and the brand backs its vermeil with a five-year warranty against plating wear, which is a real signal, not just marketing: a brand willing to guarantee its plating for five years is telling you something concrete about how it expects the piece to perform.

## £150–400: where solid gold actually starts

This is the tier where "solid gold" stops being an occasional upsell and becomes the standard offering. Mejuri's Huggie U Hoops in 10k solid yellow gold sit at $228 (roughly £180), and its Bold Small Hoops in 14k solid gold at $300 (roughly £237) sit close to Astrid & Miyu's simpler Solid Gold Simple Hoops at £230. [Astley Clarke's](https://www.astleyclarke.com) separate Solid Gold hoop line, made in recycled 14ct gold, also sits in this band, distinct from its cheaper vermeil Linia range above. Astrid & Miyu's more ornate Ridged Solid Gold Hoops move closer to £400, purely on the extra metal weight and finishing work. [Our comparison of Astrid & Miyu, Daisy London, and Otiumberg](/guides/astrid-miyu-daisy-london-otiumberg) covers Astrid & Miyu's wider range and in-store services.

At this level, weight becomes a genuine consideration alongside metal purity. A solid gold hoop at a larger diameter is a real commitment on the ear over a full day, and it's worth trying a comparable weight in person before buying a statement size sight unseen, if a store visit is available to you.

## £400 and above: built to keep

Above £400, you're generally buying solid 18ct gold, frequently with diamonds, from houses positioning the piece as a long-term investment, not a seasonal buy. Mejuri's own range reaches into this tier: its Bezel Lab Grown Diamond Huggies retail at $648 (roughly £512), and its heavier-gauge Plush Small Hoops in 14k solid gold reach $1,100 (roughly £869), which shows how far a single brand's range can stretch once diamonds or gauge weight enter the picture.

Annoushka's Eclipse hoops, handcrafted in solid 18ct yellow, rose, or white gold and set with diamonds, sit in the same territory alongside Astley Clarke's higher-end solid gold and gemstone pieces. Annoushka doesn't publish a single fixed price across its earring range, so it's worth checking current pricing directly before assuming a figure, though comparable solid 18ct diamond-set pieces from the house typically run well past £600. The jump in price at this level buys higher gold purity, better stone quality where stones are set, and a level of finishing (polished interiors, precisely fitted closures) that tends to separate fine jewellery from demi-fine even when the silhouette looks similar from across a room.

This is also the tier where resale and longevity actually matter, since an 18ct gold hoop with good stones holds intrinsic value in a way a plated or vermeil piece simply doesn't.

## What to actually check before buying

Whatever your budget, four questions are worth asking regardless of price point, since brands don't always volunteer the answers.

**What's the base metal, precisely?** "Gold" alone isn't an answer. Ask whether it's solid gold, vermeil, gold-fill, or plating, since all four get marketed under similar language and only one of them is gold all the way through.

**What's the plating thickness, if it's vermeil?** UK vermeil has a legal minimum of 2.5 microns, but some houses plate considerably thicker, which shows up as better wear over years, not months.

**Is there a warranty on plating specifically?** A brand's willingness to guarantee plated gold against wear is one of the more honest signals available, since it puts their own money behind the claim.

**Solid or hollow tube, at larger sizes?** Ask directly if it isn't stated. It affects both comfort and how the piece survives being caught on scarves, hoods, and hair over years of wear.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are gold-plated hoop earrings bad for your ears?

Not inherently. They're more likely to cause a skin reaction over time than vermeil or solid gold, because the base metal underneath the plating is more exposed as the plating wears, and that base metal is often a nickel-containing alloy. If you have sensitive ears or plan to wear a pair daily, vermeil or solid gold is the safer choice regardless of budget, since both use a hypoallergenic surface all the way through, not a thin coating over one.

### What's the difference between gold vermeil and gold-plated hoops?

Vermeil is gold plated over sterling silver, with a legally regulated minimum thickness in the UK of 2.5 microns. Standard gold plating can be applied over any base metal, including brass, at any thickness, with no minimum requirement. Vermeil costs more because it's a better-defined, thicker, more durable product, not because of marketing alone.

### How much should you spend on gold hoop earrings?

It depends on how often you plan to wear them. For occasional wear, gold-plated pieces under £50 are a reasonable choice. For daily wear against skin, gold vermeil from £50 to £150 is the honest minimum for something built to last more than a season. Solid gold, from around £150 upward depending on size and weight, is worth it specifically if you want a piece that won't need replacing in a few years.

### Do solid gold hoop earrings tarnish?

No. Solid gold does not tarnish or corrode under normal wear, which is one of the genuine practical advantages over plated or vermeil pieces, both of which can show wear at the plating over years even when properly cared for. This is one of the few cases where the more expensive option is also the lower-maintenance one.
